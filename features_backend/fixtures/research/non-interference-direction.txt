The question: can a system keep a secret from an attacker who can compromise an agent that interacts with that secret? This is about non-interference and the mathematical limits on containment. Steganography is one example, not the subject.

The adversary: an attacker five years from now who poisons the model at the centre of a system designed today. Future models will find any vulnerability that exists, however unlikely, so the question is what the mathematics allows, not how likely an attack is.

The class of system: it can reach a secret, an attacker can influence it, and an attacker can observe something it affects. Almost all modern AI systems fit.

Working thesis, to be tested and not assumed: if a compromised agent can reach a secret and the attacker can observe results, containment is bounded by a formula in which attacker skill never appears. The only levers are what the agent can reach and what the attacker can observe.

Keep these claims apart, because the sources blur them:
1. Impossibility: a compromised reader cannot be contained. Goguen and Meseguer, Lampson, and the information-theory bound (leakage per observation, Smith min-entropy).
2. Undecidability: checking arbitrary programs for non-interference. Rice, the halting reduction, termination channels (Askarov et al.), and enforcement that rejects safe programs (Sabelfeld and Myers).
3. Intractability: a specific neural network is finite, so Rice does not strictly apply.
4. Achievable: keeping the secret outside the agent's domain (the seL4 proof, and what it excludes). Declassification (Zdancewic and Myers, Rushby). Computational hardness and obfuscation limits (Barak et al.). Confused deputy.
