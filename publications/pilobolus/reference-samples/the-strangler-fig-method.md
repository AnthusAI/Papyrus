A strangler fig begins high in somebody else’s tree.

A seed lands in a fork of bark. The young plant drinks what collects there.
It sends roots down through the host toward the ground and branches up toward
the light. For years the old tree does the holding. It looks like the same
tree from the path.

Then the roots reach earth. They thicken. They close around the trunk that
carried them. The fig has its own food and its own frame. Sometimes the first
tree dies inside it. Sometimes the outline remains, hollow, with the new tree
wearing its shape.

[Martin Fowler used that plant to explain a gentler way of replacing old
software](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/StranglerFigApplication.html). Nobody
turns off the old system on Friday and hopes the new one works on Monday. A new
piece takes one small job. It sits beside the old machinery, uses the old
routes, takes another job, and keeps going. At the end the service still has
the same sign over the door. Its working parts have been changed one by one.

That is the useful question for AI: where is it being planted?

Look at a social app. It began as a place where people made profiles, posted
photographs, and found one another. The human crowd built the place’s value:
the family pictures, the neighborhood arguments, the wedding invitations, the
names in the search bar. An early recommendation system could sit inside that
world and choose which of those human things appeared first. The people stayed
in the same app. The route between them had changed.

Now the next roots are being lowered. [Meta says its AI assistant is built into
Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, Threads, and WhatsApp](https://ai.meta.com/meta-ai/assistant/).
It says the assistant can draw on information a person chose to share through
those products, including profile information and the things they like or
engage with. The company has also [put AI characters on Facebook and
Instagram](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/09/social-profiles-for-metas-ai-characters/),
and its [AI Studio lets people make characters to share across its messaging
and social apps](https://ai.meta.com/ai-studio/).

The system does not have to persuade people to move into a strange new
machine. Their friends are already there. Their history is already there. The
button is already in the room. It starts by answering a question, suggesting a
place, helping write a message, or supplying somebody to talk to. Each task
is small enough to feel like help.

The old social world keeps doing the expensive work. People reveal what they
care about. They teach the system which face, phrase, and grievance makes them
stop scrolling. They provide the familiar setting that makes an answer feel
like it came from somewhere they know. The new layer learns the paths through
that world and begins offering its own people, its own replies, and its own
version of what deserves attention.

This is the change the fig explains. The takeover does not begin when every
profile is fake. It begins when the human profile becomes the soil: useful for
training, useful for targeting, useful for lending the machine a social
setting. The app can still look full of cousins, coworkers, and old friends
while more of the meeting is arranged by a system that has no cousin, no
coworker, and no stake in the memory.

The last stage is easy to miss because the tree is still standing. A person
opens the familiar app to find another person. A system decides who appears,
offers the words, supplies the companion, and fills the quiet. The old trunk
is still there to lean on. The life inside it has moved somewhere else.
